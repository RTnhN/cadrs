# Tessellation of trimmed faces, exact mass properties and geometric queries (point projection, minimum distance, silhouettes, HLR)

Scope: published methods for the `tess`, `mass`, `distance`, `silhouette`, `hlr`, `uvdomain` and
`nurbs` modules of `cadrs_kernel::native`, with complexity/accuracy trade-offs, so the kernel team can
pick what to adopt. Where cadrs stands today (README lines 153-220, 344-362): every edge is sampled
once (chord deflection and half the angle step) and each face is triangulated by a constrained
Delaunay triangulation (spade) in metric-scaled uv, in parallel; meshing is about even with OCCT
(0.97). Mass properties use the divergence theorem per face, integrated by Green's theorem over the
uv domain with nested Gauss-Legendre (along v for ruled faces, a tensor rule on knot spans for
untrimmed B-spline patches); 0.52 of OCCT's adaptive integration to 1e-9 but 11x slower than OCCT's
fixed Gauss rules (which are 1e-3 off on the blade hubs). Min/max distance: branch and bound on
cell boxes, then damped Newton with all second derivatives. HLR: exact silhouettes and edges,
crossings refined by Newton, visibility by rays against faces.

Downloaded PDFs are in `../papers/tessellation_mass_queries/` (15 files;
each verified to start with `%PDF`). Paywalled items are cited with DOI only. Notes on method
details that I could not verify against a full text are marked "(from memory)".

## Must read (top 5)

1. **Gunderman, Weiss & Evans 2021, "High-accuracy mesh-free quadrature for trimmed parametric
   surfaces and volumes"** (CAD 141, 2021; arXiv:2101.06497). The direct upgrade path for `mass`
   on trimmed faces: the same Green's-theorem reduction cadrs already uses, but with the inner
   integral done by numerical antidifferentiation with Gauss rules, giving exponential convergence
   up to the trimming-curve approximation error. Local: `Gunderman-Weiss-Evans-2021-mesh-free-quadrature-trimmed-parametric-surfaces.pdf`.
2. **Gonzalez-Ochoa, McCammon & Peters 1998, "Computing moments of objects enclosed by piecewise
   polynomial surfaces"** (ACM TOG 17(3)). Exact (closed-form) moments for polynomial patches via
   Bernstein products; the way to make untrimmed non-rational B-spline faces free of quadrature error
   at fixed cost. Paywalled (ACM "bronze" page); 1997 Purdue TR version exists.
3. **Balázs, Guthe & Klein 2004, "Efficient trimmed NURBS tessellation"** (WSCG 2004) plus the
   **Filip, Magedson & Markot 1986** derivative bound it rests on. A complete, guaranteed-error
   trimmed-surface tessellation pipeline (3D trimming curves, Hausdorff-bounded loop simplification,
   kd-tree surface subdivision with control-point error bounds, half-edge trimming, polygon
   triangulation). Local: `Balazs-Guthe-Klein-2004-efficient-trimmed-NURBS-tessellation.pdf`.
4. **Johnson & Cohen 1998, "A framework for efficient minimum distance computations"** (ICRA 1998).
   The LUB-tree: breadth-first pruning with lower bounds from convex hulls of B-spline pieces and
   upper bounds from points on the surface, refinement by knot insertion of active regions; the
   model for replacing cadrs' cell-box branch and bound. Local: `Johnson-Cohen-1998-framework-efficient-minimum-distance.pdf`.
5. **Bénard & Hertzmann 2019, "Line Drawings from 3D Models: A Tutorial"** (Found. Trends CGV
   11(1-2); arXiv:1810.01175), read together with **Elber & Cohen 1990, "Hidden curve removal for
   free form surfaces"** (SIGGRAPH 1990). The tutorial's chapters 4 and 7 (mesh curve visibility,
   quantitative invisibility, parametric-surface contours and visibility) are the modern statement
   of exactly what `hlr`/`silhouette` do, with the singular points (cusps, bitangents) that a
   ray-per-segment visibility test must split at. Local: `Benard-Hertzmann-2019-line-drawings-from-3D-models-tutorial.pdf`.

## Key question 1: Tessellation of trimmed NURBS and analytic faces

### Takeaway
The literature splits into (a) grid/cell methods that tessellate the untrimmed patch on a step size
from derivative bounds and then cut the cells by polygonised trimming loops (Rockwood 1989, Piegl &
Richard 1995, Kumar & Manocha 1995, Balázs/Guthe/Klein 2004 with a kd-tree instead of a grid), and
(b) parameter-space triangulation methods that insert the trim polygon as constraints and refine the
interior (Sheng & Hirsch 1992, Hamann & Tsai 1996, OCCT BRepMesh, Delaunay refinement à la
Ruppert/Chew/Shewchuk, anisotropic metric meshing à la Borouchaki/Laug/George). cadrs is already in
camp (b); the gains available are a guaranteed chordal bound (Filip 1986) to drive interior point
insertion instead of (or in addition to) a fixed metric scaling, anisotropic sizing from the first
fundamental form, and GPU CDT/refinement if meshing ever becomes the bottleneck.

### Cited Findings

#### [T1] Filip, Magedson & Markot 1986 — the chordal-deviation bound
- Citation: Daniel Filip, Robert Magedson, Robert Markot. "Surface algorithms using bounds on derivatives." Computer Aided Geometric Design 3(4):295-311, 1986. DOI [10.1016/0167-8396(86)90005-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-8396(86)90005-1). Paywalled; no OA copy found. (Author list confirmed via Crossref.)
- Algorithm (from memory; standard result): for a C² curve C(t) sampled with parameter step Δt, the chord error is ≤ (1/8) Δt² max|C''|; for a surface S(u,v) approximated by triangles over a uv rectangle of size (Δu, Δv), the deviation from the bilinear/triangular interpolant is ≤ (1/8)(Δu² M_uu + 2 Δu Δv M_uv + Δv² M_vv) with M = sup of the second partials over the domain. Bounds on the second partials come from differences of control points (hodographs); for rational surfaces the bound is applied to the homogeneous form or via the quotient rule. Given a tolerance ε one solves for the largest (Δu, Δv) that satisfy the bound, giving the number of uniform steps per direction (or per knot span). The paper also uses the bounds for subdivision-based intersection and for deciding "flat enough".
- Limitations: the bound is global over the domain (per span if applied per span), so it over-refines where curvature is concentrated; it is for uniform steps, not adaptive meshes; it needs derivative bounds, which are loose for rational surfaces with uneven weights.
- Relevance to cadrs: `tess` (interior point density per face, instead of a purely metric-scaled CDT) and the edge sampler (replace the chord/angle sampling loop by a one-shot step count on B-spline spans). Also `mass` (choosing the Gauss order from derivative magnitude) and `distance` (cell-box tightness).

#### [T2] Rockwood, Heaton & Davis 1989 — real-time trimmed surfaces ("coving")
- Citation: Alyn Rockwood, Kurt Heaton, Tom Davis. "Real-time rendering of trimmed surfaces." ACM SIGGRAPH Computer Graphics 23(3):107-116, 1989. DOI [10.1145/74334.74344](https://doi.org/10.1145/74334.74344) (Crossref confirms). Paywalled; no OA copy found.
- Algorithm (abstract + from memory): the rational tensor-product patch is split into Bézier sub-patches, each tessellated uniformly with a step derived from a view-dependent flatness test; trimming curves are segmented in parameter space to the same tolerance; the trimmed region is decomposed into uv-monotone regions; the strip between the trimming polyline and the interior uniform grid is filled by "coving" (triangle fans connecting the boundary polyline to the grid rows), so the interior is regular and only a one-cell-wide band is irregular; adjacent patches share boundary step counts to avoid cracks.
- Limitations: visualization-oriented (view-dependent steps); coving makes slivers near the boundary; no guaranteed 3D bound on trim-curve error.
- Relevance: `tess` — historic baseline; the "regular interior + irregular band" idea is what OCCT's range splitters do.

#### [T3] Sheng & Hirsch 1992 — triangulation of trimmed surfaces in parametric space
- Citation: X. Sheng, B. E. Hirsch. "Triangulation of trimmed surfaces in parametric space." Computer-Aided Design 24(8):437-444, 1992. DOI [10.1016/0010-4485(92)90011-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-4485(92)90011-X) (Crossref confirms). Paywalled.
- Algorithm (secondary description from a citing paper, plus memory): the trimming loops are polygonised in parameter space with a 3D tolerance mapped to uv step sizes using derivative bounds (Filip-style, per parametric direction); the resulting polygonal regions (multiply connected) are stored in a half-edge structure and triangulated by a *restricted* Delaunay triangulation (constrained to the polygon edges); interior nodes are inserted on a uv grid whose spacing also comes from the derivative bounds. Triangles are then mapped back to 3D.
- Limitations: isotropic in uv per direction only (a grid), not curvature-adaptive within a span; sliver triangles where grid nodes fall close to the trim polygon (a known weakness that Piegl & Richard addressed).
- Relevance: `tess` — this is essentially cadrs' current design; the point of reading it is the uv step derivation.

#### [T4] Piegl & Richard 1995 — tessellating trimmed NURBS surfaces
- Citation: Les A. Piegl, Arnaud M. Richard. "Tessellating trimmed NURBS surfaces." Computer-Aided Design 27(1):16-26, 1995. DOI [10.1016/0010-4485(95)90749-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-4485(95)90749-6) (title/volume confirmed from Piegl's publication list and Semantic Scholar). Paywalled; no OA copy.
- Algorithm (from memory): trimming curves are polygonised to a 3D tolerance; the surface domain is cut into cells by the knot lines and further by a step from curvature/derivative bounds; each cell entirely inside the trimmed region yields two triangles; cells crossed by trimming polygons are clipped (polygon clipping in uv) and the pieces triangulated by a simple polygon triangulator; special care is taken to avoid tiny slivers by snapping polygon vertices close to cell lines. Their 1998 follow-up (Piegl & Tiller, "Geometry-based triangulation of trimmed NURBS surfaces", CAD 30(1):11-18) moves the decisions into 3D: points are placed by 3D chord/curvature criteria and the triangulation is done with a 3D-aware criterion to avoid the uv-distortion problems of parameter-space Delaunay.
- Limitations: cell clipping produces many small triangles along trims; the 1995 method uses a uniform (per span) step; cracks between faces unless edge polylines are shared.
- Relevance: `tess` — the alternative to CDT when uv distortion makes Delaunay-in-uv give bad 3D triangles (the 1998 paper is the one that argues for 3D criteria; cadrs' metric scaling is a partial answer).

#### [T5] Kumar & Manocha 1995 — efficient rendering of trimmed NURBS; super-surfaces
- Citation: Subodh Kumar, Dinesh Manocha. "Efficient rendering of trimmed NURBS surfaces." Computer-Aided Design 27(7):509-521, 1995. Abstract page: https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/RENDER/abs.trim.html (PostScript by ftp, no PDF). Related: Kumar, Manocha, Lastra, "Interactive display of large NURBS models", IEEE TVCG 2(4), 1996 (tech report page https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/RENDER/abs.tech-8.html).
- Algorithm (abstract + from memory): NURBS → Bézier patches and trimming curves → Bézier curves; per patch a uniform step in u and v is computed from screen-space tolerance via derivative bounds (Filip); trimming curves are tessellated to the same tolerance; the cells cut by trims are triangulated; incremental (frame-to-frame coherent) re-tessellation; cracks between adjacent patches of different step are fixed by "super-surfaces": groups of patches whose boundary tessellations are made to match at run time (requires adjacency clustering as preprocessing).
- Limitations: visualization; the super-surface stitching needs a priori connectivity; uniform per-patch steps.
- Relevance: `tess` — the crack-free boundary strategy is the one cadrs already has (one sampling per edge); the useful part is the step derivation and Bézier-piece organisation.

#### [T6] Hamann & Tsai 1996 — arbitrary trimming curves
- Citation: Bernd Hamann, P.-Y. Tsai. "A tessellation algorithm for the representation of trimmed NURBS surfaces with arbitrary trimming curves." Computer-Aided Design 28(6-7):461-472, 1996. OA (eScholarship, UC Davis): https://escholarship.org/uc/item/04n8h0b9 — local `Hamann-Tsai-1996-tessellation-trimmed-NURBS-arbitrary-trimming-curves.pdf` (13-page scan; text layer only on the cover page).
- Algorithm (from memory of the paper): the method works in parameter space with arbitrary (nested, multiply connected) trimming loops; trimming curves are approximated by polygons under a curvature-based criterion; the interior is triangulated by inserting points where the surface's curvature demands and performing Delaunay-style edge flips; a triangle-quality criterion in 3D guides refinement.
- Limitations: no formal error bound; scanned PDF (hard to search).
- Relevance: `tess` — background only.

#### [T7] Balázs, Guthe & Klein 2004 — efficient trimmed NURBS tessellation with guaranteed error (MUST READ)
- Citation: Ákos Balázs, Michael Guthe, Reinhard Klein. "Efficient trimmed NURBS tessellation." Journal of WSCG 12(1-3):27-33, 2004. Handle: https://dspace5.zcu.cz/handle/11025/1747 ; PDF https://dspace5.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/1747/1/I29.pdf — local `Balazs-Guthe-Klein-2004-efficient-trimmed-NURBS-tessellation.pdf` (verified against the PDF).
- Algorithm (from the PDF, sections 3-8): (1) Each 2D trimming curve is cut at the surface's knot spans and lifted to an *exact* 3D Bézier curve of degree d2d·(du+dv) by evaluating d3d+1 points and solving the Bernstein interpolation system by SVD, then losslessly degree-reduced (Farin's algorithm). (2) The 3D trimming loops are approximated by polylines using the convex-hull bound ε_line ≤ (1 − 1/2^(n−2)) max_i |(P_i − P_0) − λ(P_n − P_0)| and midpoint subdivision until the bound is under a fixed fraction γ of the tolerance; a Hausdorff-bounded line simplification then removes redundant vertices with (1−γ) of the tolerance (γ = 3/4 worked best); intersecting loops are repaired by a sweep. (3) The untrimmed surface is subdivided by a kd-tree (split in u or v, at the control point farthest from the bilinear approximation) until a conservative error bound holds: ε ≤ ε_bilin + ¼|P00 − Pm0 − P0n + Pmn| with ε_bilin = max_ij |P_ij − bilinear(P corners)| (from Kahlesz et al. 2002); an optional cheaper "approximate" bound replaces P_ij by the surface at the Greville-like parameters. (4) Trimming is done by inserting the trim polylines as directed half-edges into the kd-tree mesh in parameter space and walking the half-edge graph to extract the kept polygons (pseudo-code in section 6). (5) The polygons are triangulated, and (6) the surface is evaluated at the mesh vertices (direct evaluation is fastest per their survey).
- Limitations: the surface error bound is a control-net bound and overestimates (they say "sometimes significant"); kd-tree cells and trims produce polygons that still need a robust polygon triangulator; the 3D-trim-curve lifting has degree up to 3·(du+dv) (e.g. 18 for bicubic × cubic trims) and needs SVD; designed for rendering of many patches, not for watertightness across faces (that came in their 2003/2005 GPU work).
- Relevance: `tess` (guaranteed-error edge sampling via the convex-hull bound on the *3D* trim curve, not just the chord test; kd-tree subdivision as the interior-point generator for B-spline faces, anisotropic by construction since it splits in u or v) and `nurbs` (lifting p-curves to exact 3D Bézier curves is the same composition cadrs needs for exact edges on B-spline faces).

#### [T8] Guthe, Balázs & Klein 2005 — GPU-based trimming and tessellation
- Citation: Michael Guthe, Ákos Balázs, Reinhard Klein. "GPU-based trimming and tessellation of NURBS and T-Spline surfaces." ACM Transactions on Graphics 24(3):1016-1023 (SIGGRAPH 2005). DOI [10.1145/1073204.1073305](https://doi.org/10.1145/1073204.1073305) (Crossref confirms). Paywalled; no OA copy found (Bonn CG site blocked the fetch).
- Algorithm (from memory): each patch is approximated by bicubic Bézier sub-patches with an error bound; a per-patch tessellation level (power of two) is chosen from the bound and the view; the GPU vertex shader evaluates the bicubic patches on a uniform grid; trimming is done per fragment by sampling a trim texture (the trimming loops rasterised in parameter space, with Bézier curve evaluation in the shader to get sub-texel accuracy); cracks between patches are hidden by matching boundary levels / fillets.
- Limitations: pixel-accurate rendering only; trim texture resolution limits accuracy; not a mesh you can export.
- Relevance: `tess` — only if a GPU display path is wanted; not for export meshes.

#### [T9] Schollmeyer & Fröhlich 2009 — direct trimming of NURBS on the GPU
- Citation: Andre Schollmeyer, Bernd Fröhlich. "Direct trimming of NURBS surfaces on the GPU." ACM Transactions on Graphics 28(3), article 47 (SIGGRAPH 2009). DOI [10.1145/1531326.1531353](https://doi.org/10.1145/1531326.1531353) (Crossref confirms). Follow-up: "Efficient and anti-aliased trimming for rendering large NURBS models", IEEE TVCG 2019 (Weimar VR group page: https://uni-weimar.de/de/medien/professuren/medieninformatik/vr/research/real-time-rendering/gpucast/efficient-and-anti-aliased-trimming). Paywalled.
- Algorithm (from memory): the trimming loops are split into uv-monotone Bézier segments stored in a two-level kd-tree-like structure in parameter space (partition in v, then in u); for each fragment the shader classifies the point by counting crossings of a horizontal ray with the monotone curve segments, locating the curve crossing by Bézier clipping/bisection; no polygonal approximation of the trims, so trims are exact to pixel precision; surfaces are rendered by tessellation or ray casting.
- Limitations: rendering only; per-fragment cost grows with trim complexity.
- Relevance: `tess`/display — the monotone-segment classification structure is also a good point-in-trimmed-domain test for `uvdomain` on the CPU.

#### [T10] Abi-Ezzi & Subramaniam 1994 — fast dynamic tessellation
- Citation: Salim Abi-Ezzi, Srikanth Subramaniam. "Fast dynamic tessellation of trimmed NURBS surfaces." Computer Graphics Forum 13(3):107-126 (Eurographics 1994). DOI [10.1111/1467-8659.1330107](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8659.1330107). EG digital library record https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/6894 (PDF not freely downloadable).
- Algorithm (from memory): pipeline-oriented; trimming loops are preprocessed into monotone pieces, the patch into Bézier pieces with precomputed derivative bounds; at run time each piece's step is derived from the view, and the trimmed cells tessellated; boundary steps are made consistent to avoid cracks.
- Relevance: `tess` background.

#### [T11] OpenCASCADE BRepMesh (BRepMesh_IncrementalMesh / IMeshTools)
- Sources: OCCT mesh user guide https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__mesh.html (fetched); forum notes https://occt3d.com/dev/content/brepmeshincremental-mesh-algorithm/index.html (fetched); refman https://dev.opencascade.org/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_mesh___incremental_mesh.html.
- Algorithm (as documented): six stages. (1) Model creation: the shape is exploded into faces and edges, each edge keeping its 3D curve and one pcurve per adjacent face. (2) Edge discretisation: "each edge is processed separately, the adjacency is not taken into account"; existing polygons are reused if they meet the parameters; criteria are linear deflection ("limits the distance between a curve and its tessellation") and angular deflection ("limits the angle between subsequent segments"), with a relative mode scaling deflection by edge size. (3) Model healing: "forcibly connects the ends of adjacent edges in the parametric space"; self-intersections resolved by refining edges up to five times or splitting, else the face is refused. (4) Preprocessing: existing triangulations validated. (5) Face discretisation: "each face is processed separately, thus it is possible to process faces in a parallel mode"; the algorithm per face is a Delaunay triangulation of the boundary nodes in uv with Watson's (Bowyer–Watson) insertion (forum answer by K. Gavrilov: "BRepMesh computes Delaunay's triangulation with the algorithm of Watson ... splits triangles until result triangulation satisfies deflection criteria"); internal nodes come from surface-type "range splitters" (default: none; cylinder/cone/sphere/torus: analytic iso-lines; NURBS: uv-based from knots and deflection), and `ControlSurfaceDeflection` runs an "additional procedure controlling deflection of generated triangles" that inserts nodes where the triangle-to-surface distance exceeds the deflection. (6) Post-processing commits polygons to edges (`Poly_PolygonOnTriangulation`). Parameters: Deflection, Angle (12-20 degrees recommended), Relative, DeflectionInterior/AngleInterior (B-spline faces only), MinSize, InternalVerticesMode, ControlSurfaceDeflection, InParallel. Watertightness follows from discretising each edge once and sharing the polygon between the two faces (face triangulations are stored per face; "a closed mesh exists only when combined").
- Limitations: fails on some faces (null triangulation), no guarantee of closure for invalid shells; uv Delaunay with a crude metric (from memory: BRepMesh scales uv by iso-line lengths before the Delaunay); deflection control is a posteriori insertion, so it iterates.
- Relevance: `tess` — cadrs' design is the same; meshing parity (0.97) suggests the remaining differences are in interior-node generation per surface type (analytic splitters are cheap and give regular meshes on cylinders/tori) and in the deflection-control loop.

#### [T12] Delaunay refinement: Chew 1989/1993, Ruppert 1995, Shewchuk 1996/2002
- Citations: L. Paul Chew, "Guaranteed-quality triangular meshes", Cornell TR 89-983, 1989; Chew, "Guaranteed-quality mesh generation for curved surfaces", Proc. 9th SoCG 1993, pp. 274-280, DOI [10.1145/160985.161150](https://doi.org/10.1145/160985.161150); Jim Ruppert, "A Delaunay refinement algorithm for quality 2-dimensional mesh generation", J. Algorithms 18(3):548-585, 1995, DOI [10.1006/jagm.1995.1021](https://doi.org/10.1006/jagm.1995.1021); Jonathan R. Shewchuk, "Triangle: engineering a 2D quality mesh generator and Delaunay triangulator", WACG 1996 (LNCS 1148), OA https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/papers/triangle.pdf — local `Shewchuk-1996-Triangle.pdf`; Shewchuk, "Delaunay refinement algorithms for triangular mesh generation", Computational Geometry: Theory and Applications 22(1-3):21-74, 2002, DOI [10.1016/S0925-7721(01)00047-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-7721(01)00047-5), OA https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/papers/2dj.pdf — local `Shewchuk-2002-Delaunay-refinement-triangular-mesh-generation.pdf`. Book: Cheng, Dey, Shewchuk, *Delaunay Mesh Generation*, CRC 2012 (https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/meshbook.html).
- Algorithm (from the 2002 paper's abstract and body): start from the CDT of the input PSLG; repeatedly insert the circumcentre of any triangle whose circumradius-to-shortest-edge ratio exceeds a bound B (equivalently, minimum angle below arcsin(1/2B)), or that violates a user size field; if a circumcentre would encroach a constrained segment's diametral circle, split the segment at its midpoint instead (Ruppert); Chew's variant inserts off-centres/uses the constrained Delaunay "diametral lens". Guarantees: no angle below ~20.7° (Ruppert) or ~26.5°-30° (Chew's second algorithm), size-optimal grading, termination; the 2002 paper unifies both analyses and handles small input angles (concentric-shell segment splitting, "terminator"). Triangle implements it with Shewchuk's adaptive-precision exact predicates (orientation, incircle), which spade also uses. Chew 1993 applies the same refinement directly on a curved surface: the Delaunay triangulation is computed with respect to surface distances/the restricted Delaunay, circumcentres are projected to the surface, which gives a surface mesh with angle bounds and no slivers in 3D.
- Limitations: quality criteria are in the plane where the triangulation lives (uv), unless Chew's curved-surface variant or a metric is used; a size field (from curvature) must be supplied for chordal accuracy; worst-case O(n²) insertions but practical O(n log n).
- Relevance: `tess` — turn cadrs' CDT into a *refinement* with a sizing function from the Filip bound (or from the deflection check as OCCT does): insert circumcentres only in triangles that fail the deflection test or the angle bound. With spade already in place this is a small step (spade has a `refine` API with angle/size limits in recent versions).

#### [T13] Robust CDT libraries and GPU CDT/refinement (2012-2023)
- Qi, Cao & Tan, "Computing 2D constrained Delaunay triangulation using the GPU", I3D 2012 (DOI [10.1145/2159616.2159623](https://doi.org/10.1145/2159616.2159623)) and IEEE TVCG 19(5):736-748, 2013 (DOI [10.1109/TVCG.2012.307](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2012.307)); OA tech report TRB3/11 https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/cdt_files/TRB3-11-report.pdf — local `Qi-Cao-Tan-2012-GPU-constrained-Delaunay-triangulation.pdf`. Algorithm (from the PDF): Phase 1 map points to a texture and compute a *digital* Voronoi diagram (jump flooding); derive an approximate triangulation from it; fix it into a true Delaunay triangulation by parallel edge flipping with exact predicates; then insert constraints by parallel flipping of edges crossing each constraint; missing points (several per pixel) are inserted afterwards. Claims up to an order of magnitude over Triangle on millions of points. Chen, Qi & Tan, "Computing Delaunay refinement using the GPU", I3D 2017 (DOI [10.1145/3023368.3023373](https://doi.org/10.1145/3023368.3023373)); OA https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~tants/gqm_files/11-0018-chen.pdf — local `Chen-Qi-Tan-2017-GPU-Delaunay-refinement.pdf`: parallel Steiner-point insertion with conflict detection; proven termination for input angles ≥ 60° and θ ≤ 20.7°; code https://github.com/chenzhenghai/GPU_CDT_Refine.
- Hu, Schneider, Gao, Zhou, Jacobson, Zorin, Panozzo, "TriWild: robust triangulation with curve constraints", ACM TOG 38(4), article 52, 2019, DOI [10.1145/3306346.3323011](https://doi.org/10.1145/3306346.3323011); code https://github.com/wildmeshing/triwild ; project page https://research.adobe.com/publication/triwild-robust-triangulation-with-curve-constraints (the NSF PAR copy https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10132346 downloaded as a corrupt PDF twice, so no local copy). Algorithm (from the abstract/project page and memory of the paper): the input is a set of possibly intersecting/overlapping curves (SVG-like); the algorithm first builds a conforming linear mesh with an ε-envelope tolerance (features are preserved within a distance envelope, not exactly), then makes the triangles *curved* (cubic Bézier edges) so that the mesh boundary reproduces the smooth feature curves, with local operations (split, collapse, swap, smooth) keeping validity/injectivity under the envelope. This is the "robust constrained Delaunay by Hu et al." I assume the brief meant; it is not a CDT in the strict sense (it is envelope-based and tolerant of bad input).
- Diazzi, Panozzo, Vaxman & Attene, "Constrained Delaunay tetrahedrization: a robust and practical approach", ACM TOG 42(6), 2023, DOI [10.1145/3618352](https://doi.org/10.1145/3618352) (3D, exact arithmetic; listed for completeness).
- Rust/C++ CDT libraries: spade (used by cadrs; robust predicates, CDT, refinement) https://github.com/Stoeoef/spade ; `cdt` crate (Formlabs; exact predicates) https://docs.rs/cdt ; detria (C++17, Shewchuk predicates) https://github.com/Kimbatt/detria.
- Relevance: `tess` — GPU CDT only pays off for millions of points; for CAD faces (hundreds to thousands of nodes) the per-face parallelism cadrs already has is the right level. TriWild matters if cadrs ever wants *curved* uv triangles that follow p-curves exactly (e.g. for display of trims at any zoom), or a tolerant mesher for healed/imperfect loops.

#### [T14] Anisotropic / metric-based surface meshing in parameter space
- Citation: Houman Borouchaki, Patrick Laug, Paul-Louis George. "Parametric surface meshing using a combined advancing-front generalized Delaunay approach." Int. J. Numer. Meth. Engng 49(1-2):233-259, 2000. DOI [10.1002/1097-0207(20000910/20)49:1/2<233::AID-NME931>3.0.CO;2-G](https://doi.org/10.1002/1097-0207(20000910/20)49:1/2<233::AID-NME931>3.0.CO;2-G) (Crossref confirms). Paywalled. Related OA: Laug & Borouchaki, "Surface meshing with metric gradation control", Civil-Comp 2012, https://webapp.tudelft.nl/proceedings/ect2012/pdf/laug.pdf (download failed; the page exists). Also Pascal J. Frey, Houman Borouchaki, "Geometric surface mesh optimization", Computing and Visualization in Science 1(3):113-121, 1998; Frey & George, *Mesh Generation: Application to Finite Elements*, 2nd ed., Wiley 2008; Boissonnat, Shi, Tournois, Yvinec, "Anisotropic Delaunay meshes of surfaces", ACM TOG 34(2), 2015, DOI [10.1145/2721895](https://doi.org/10.1145/2721895).
- Algorithm (from memory; this is the "indirect approach" the 2012 abstract describes): define at every uv point a 2×2 Riemannian metric M(u,v) = JᵀJ (first fundamental form, J = [S_u S_v]) so that lengths measured in M equal 3D lengths, and intersect it with a *geometric* size metric derived from curvature: in principal direction i the allowed edge length is h_i = sqrt(8 ε / κ_i) (chord error ε), i.e. the metric is anisotropic where κ₁ ≠ κ₂ (cylinders: long edges along the rulings). Mesh the uv domain with an advancing-front/Delaunay generator where "unit length" is measured in the local metric (lengths by metric interpolation along edges), then optimise (edge swaps by metric Delaunay criterion, smoothing, gradation control so neighbouring sizes do not jump more than a ratio). This is the algorithm behind BLSURF/MeshGems-CADSurf (Laug & Borouchaki), used as a SALOME/OCCT mesher plugin.
- Limitations: needs curvature at every point (cheap for cadrs' surfaces); metric-Delaunay is not a true Delaunay (no global optimality), so the flip loop must be careful; gradation control adds passes.
- Relevance: `tess` — the principled version of cadrs' "metric-scaled uv": instead of one scaling per face, use a pointwise metric; a cylinder or torus then gets long thin triangles along the straight direction without losing the chord bound. Boissonnat et al. 2015 is the theoretical anisotropic-Delaunay reference if one wants guarantees.

#### [T15] Watertightness across shared edges
- Findings: every method that produces export meshes relies on discretising each edge once and sharing the polyline (OCCT [T11]; cadrs today); rendering-only methods instead stitch (Kumar's super-surfaces [T5]; Guthe, Meseth, Klein's "seam graph", Pacific Graphics 2002; Balázs, Guthe, Klein 2003 GPU crack filling, both cited in [T7] section 2). No newer general result: the problem is solved by construction when the edge is the shared primitive.
- Relevance: `tess` — nothing to change; keep edges as the shared primitive and never re-sample a p-curve per face. The only subtlety the papers raise is that the edge polyline's chord error must be measured on the 3D curve, and a face must be able to accept a boundary polyline denser than its interior needs.

### Inferences
- cadrs' CDT-in-uv design is the mainstream (Sheng & Hirsch, OCCT, BLSURF's indirect approach); parity with OCCT is expected. The two documented levers for both speed and quality are (1) analytic interior-node generators per surface type (cylinder/cone/sphere/torus iso-lines, as OCCT's range splitters), which produce regular meshes without a deflection-control iteration, and (2) a pointwise anisotropic metric [T14] instead of a per-face scaling.
- A guaranteed chordal bound for export meshes is available from [T1] (per span) or [T7] (per kd-cell from control points); either can be the sizing field for Delaunay refinement [T12] with spade's refinement, replacing any a-posteriori deflection check.
- GPU tessellation work (T8, T9, T13) is for rendering or for inputs orders of magnitude larger than CAD faces; not a priority for the kernel.

## Key question 2: Exact mass properties (volume, centroid, inertia, surface area)

### Takeaway
All practical B-rep methods are divergence-theorem methods (Timmer & Stern 1980; Lee & Requicha 1982 survey); the question is only how the per-face surface integral is evaluated. Three regimes: polynomial (non-rational, untrimmed) patches can be integrated *exactly* in closed form (Gonzalez-Ochoa/McCammon/Peters 1998) or by a tensor Gauss rule of order matched to the integrand degree per knot span (exact too); trimmed/rational faces need Green's theorem on the uv domain with a high-order rule along the trimming curves plus numerical antidifferentiation (Gunderman/Weiss/Evans 2021: exponential convergence, with the error dominated only by the trim-curve approximation); polyhedral faces are closed form (Mirtich 1996, Eberly 2002, Cattani & Paoluzzi 1990). OCCT's speed comes from fixed, low-order Gauss rules over the whole face domain (no knot-span splitting unless `IsUseSpan`), which is why it is 1e-3 off on B-spline faces; the way to get its speed *with* an error bound is degree-matched tensor rules per span on interior cells and Gunderman-style boundary integrals only on cells cut by trims.

### Cited Findings

#### [M1] Timmer & Stern 1980 — the divergence/Green's theorem formulation
- Citation: H. G. Timmer, J. M. Stern. "Computation of global geometric properties of solid objects." Computer-Aided Design 12(6):301-304, November 1980. (Cited by Lee & Requicha; DOI not confirmed — Crossref lookup of my guess failed.) Paywalled; no OA copy.
- Algorithm (secondary: Mortenson's *Geometric Modeling* chapter "Global properties of complex solids" says the Timmer–Stern method relies on "the divergence and Green's theorems" to turn volume integrals into surface integrals; details from memory): the volume integral ∫_V f dV is written as ∫_S F·n dS with ∇·F = f; on each parametric face F·n dS = F(S(u,v))·(S_u × S_v) du dv; the double integral over the trimmed uv domain is turned by Green's theorem into a line integral around the trimming loops in uv of a function G(u,v) with ∂G/∂u (or ∂G/∂v) equal to the integrand; G is itself obtained by one-dimensional quadrature along iso-lines from a reference line to the loop point; the line integral is evaluated by Gauss quadrature along each p-curve. This is exactly cadrs' current `mass` scheme.
- Limitations: nested quadrature cost (outer points on loops × inner points per iso-line), no error bound unless adaptive; cost grows with the number of loop pieces.
- Relevance: `mass` — the baseline; everything below is about doing the inner/outer integrals with fewer points for a given error.

#### [M2] Lee & Requicha 1982 — survey and cellular methods
- Citation: Yong Tsui Lee, Aristides A. G. Requicha. "Algorithms for computing the volume and other integral properties of solids. I. Known methods and open issues." Communications of the ACM 25(9):635-641, 1982, DOI [10.1145/358628.358643](https://doi.org/10.1145/358628.358643); "II. A family of algorithms based on representation conversion and cellular approximation", CACM 25(9):642-650, 1982 (DOI of part II not confirmed). Paywalled (ACM page https://cacm.acm.org/research/algorithms-for-computing-the-volume-and-other-integral-properties-of-solids-ii-a-family-of-algorithms-based-on-representation-conversion-and-cellular-approximation).
- Content (from the abstract and memory): part I classifies methods: direct volume integration, divergence-theorem methods (Timmer & Stern), cellular approximation (octrees, ray casting with "ray representations"), and discusses accuracy/robustness open issues; part II gives cellular algorithms for CSG with error bounds from the cell size.
- Relevance: `mass` — background; the ray-casting approach is the robust fallback for self-intersecting or invalid shells (classification is exact per ray), with O(1/h) accuracy.

#### [M3] Cattani & Paoluzzi 1990 — boundary integration over linear polyhedra
- Citation: Carlo Cattani, Alberto Paoluzzi. "Boundary integration over linear polyhedra." Computer-Aided Design 22(2):130-135, 1990. (DBLP record; DOI not confirmed.) Generalised by F. Bernardini, "Integration of polynomials over n-dimensional polyhedra", CAD 23(1):51-58, 1991. Paywalled.
- Algorithm (from memory): integrals of monomials x^a y^b z^c over a polyhedron are reduced by the divergence theorem to faces, by Stokes to edges, and finally to closed-form sums over the vertices of each (triangulated) face using multinomial expansions, giving exact formulas (rational arithmetic possible). Rathod & Govinda Rao 1998 (ANZIAM J.) give the same reduction with a Gauss divergence derivation.
- Relevance: `mass` for `meshsolid` bodies and planar faces (polygons with straight edges): exact and cheaper than quadrature.

#### [M4] Mirtich 1996 and Eberly 2002 — polyhedral mass properties
- Citations: Brian Mirtich. "Fast and accurate computation of polyhedral mass properties." Journal of Graphics Tools 1(2):31-50, 1996. DOI [10.1080/10867651.1996.10487458](https://doi.org/10.1080/10867651.1996.10487458). Author page with PostScript paper and C code: https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jfc/mirtich/massProps.html (paper `papers/volInt.ps`, code `code/volumeIntegration.tar`; no PDF, so not downloaded). David Eberly. "Polyhedral mass properties (revisited)." Geometric Tools technical document, 2002 (rev. 2009), CC-BY 4.0, https://www.geometrictools.com/Documentation/PolyhedralMassProperties.pdf — local `Eberly-2002-polyhedral-mass-properties.pdf`.
- Algorithm (from Eberly's PDF, which restates Mirtich): the ten integrals (1, x, y, z, x², y², z², xy, yz, zx) over the volume are converted by the divergence theorem with F chosen per monomial (table in the PDF: 1 → (x,0,0), x → (x²/2,0,0), x² → (x³/3,0,0), xy → (x²y/2,0,0), ...); Mirtich projects each face onto the coordinate plane that maximises the normal component (robustness) and applies Green's theorem to get line integrals of univariate polynomials along projected edges, sharing subexpressions; Eberly shows that for *triangular* faces the face integrals have direct closed forms in the vertex coordinates (no projection, no Green's theorem) and "require significantly less computational time". Both do one pass over the faces.
- Limitations: polyhedra only; precision loss for very large coordinates (centre the model first).
- Relevance: `mass` on `meshsolid` and on any planar face with straight edges; also the exact reference for mass tests.

#### [M5] Gonzalez-Ochoa, McCammon & Peters 1998 — exact moments of piecewise polynomial surfaces (MUST READ)
- Citation: Carlos Gonzalez-Ochoa, Scott McCammon, Jörg Peters. "Computing moments of objects enclosed by piecewise polynomial surfaces." ACM Transactions on Graphics 17(3):143-157, July 1998. DOI [10.1145/285857.285858](https://doi.org/10.1145/285857.285858) (Semantic Scholar lists the ACM PDF as "bronze" free-to-read: https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/285857.285858 — the download was blocked by ACM's bot check, so no local copy). Tech report version: "Computing moments of piecewise polynomial surfaces", Purdue CS TR 97-009, 1997, https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cstech/1348 (PDF https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2347&context=cstech, also blocked by a bot check). Related: J. Peters, A. Nasri, "Computing volumes of solids enclosed by recursive subdivision surfaces", Computer Graphics Forum 16(3), 1997 (Peters' publication list https://www.cise.ufl.edu/research/SurfLab/papers/peters-jorg.html).
- Algorithm (from memory of the TOG paper): for a closed surface made of polynomial tensor-product patches p(u,v) of bidegree (d,d) in Bernstein–Bézier form, each moment ∫_V x^a y^b z^c dV is written by the divergence theorem as a sum over patches of ∫∫ G(p)·(p_u × p_v) du dv where G is a polynomial vector field; the integrand is a polynomial in (u,v) of bidegree ((a+b+c+1)d + 2d − 1) in each variable whose Bernstein coefficients are products of the patch's control-point coordinates; using the product formula for Bernstein polynomials (B_i^m B_j^n = [C(m,i)C(n,j)/C(m+n,i+j)] B_{i+j}^{m+n}) and ∫_0^1 B_i^n = 1/(n+1), the moment becomes a multilinear form in the control points with coefficients that depend only on the degrees and are precomputed once; evaluation is then a fixed number of multiply-adds per patch (they tabulate the cost and show it is competitive with low-order Gauss while being exact to rounding). Subdivision surfaces are handled by converting regular patches to polynomial form and bounding the contribution of the irregular remainder; the Purdue TR is the five-page core of the same derivation.
- Limitations: polynomial (non-rational) patches only — rational NURBS are not covered (the integrand is rational); untrimmed patches only (a trimmed face needs its domain); coefficient tables grow with degree (bicubic is cheap; degree 7+ tables are large but still constant per degree).
- Relevance: `mass` — replace the "tensor rule on knot spans for untrimmed B-spline patches" by exact Bernstein-product evaluation for non-rational faces (convert each span to Bézier form, which cadrs can do by knot insertion), and use it as the exact reference in tests. For rational faces keep Gauss but note the degree argument in [M8].

#### [M6] Mørken 1991; Vermeulen, Bartels & Heppler 1992; Patrizi & Sestini 2026 — products and integrals of B-splines
- Citations: Knut Mørken. "Some identities for products and degree raising of splines." Constructive Approximation 7(1):195-208, 1991. DOI [10.1007/BF01888153](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01888153) (paywalled). A. H. Vermeulen, R. H. Bartels, G. R. Heppler. "Integrating products of B-splines." SIAM J. Sci. Stat. Comput. 13(4):1025-1038, 1992. DOI [10.1137/0913060](https://doi.org/10.1137/0913060) (paywalled; a course-page copy exists at https://web.math.pmf.unizg.hr/~rogina/CAGD/IntegratingProductsOfBsplines.pdf but it is not the publisher's or author's own copy, so not downloaded). Francesco Patrizi, Alessandra Sestini. "An algorithmic approach to direct spline products: procedures and computational aspects." arXiv:2601.17432, 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17432 — local `Patrizi-Sestini-2026-direct-spline-products-Morken-formula.pdf`.
- Algorithm: Mørken's Theorem 3.1 gives the B-spline coefficients of the product of two splines on the merged knot vector as a sum over subsets of indices of products of the factors' coefficients (restated in Patrizi & Sestini, who turn it into an Oslo-algorithm-based procedure with term factorisation and report large cost reductions versus naive evaluation, and show collocation-based alternatives can be ill-conditioned). Vermeulen et al. evaluate ∫ of a product of B-splines by integration by parts, reducing it to evaluations of a higher-order spline (exact, O(degree) per integral). SISL's `s1244` integrates products of B-spline basis functions by Gauss quadrature per knot interval (exact for total order < 12).
- Relevance: `mass`/`nurbs` — the B-spline (rather than Bézier) route to the same exactness as [M5] without converting to Bézier spans: the integrand x·(S_u × S_v) is a product of three splines, whose B-spline coefficients Mørken's formula gives directly, and ∫ of a spline is the sum of its coefficients times knot differences/(degree+1). For cadrs the Bézier-per-span route [M5] is simpler; Mørken is the reference if products of splines are needed elsewhere (e.g. exact silhouette functions n·w as a spline, as Elber does).

#### [M7] Gunderman, Weiss & Evans 2021 — high-accuracy mesh-free quadrature for trimmed parametric surfaces (MUST READ)
- Citation: David Gunderman, Kenneth Weiss, John A. Evans. "High-accuracy mesh-free quadrature for trimmed parametric surfaces and volumes." Computer-Aided Design 141:103093, 2021. arXiv:2101.06497 (v2, Jan 2022) https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.06497 — local `Gunderman-Weiss-Evans-2021-mesh-free-quadrature-trimmed-parametric-surfaces.pdf` (verified against the PDF). Predecessor (planar regions bounded by rational curves): Gunderman, Weiss, Evans, "Spectral mesh-free quadrature for planar regions bounded by rational parametric curves", CAD 130, 2021. Code: QuaHOG (MATLAB), referenced in the paper.
- Algorithm (from the PDF): two components. (1) "Iteratively reduce the dimensionality of integration using the generalized Stokes theorem to line integrals over trimming curves": for a surface integral over a trimmed patch, Green's theorem in the uv domain turns ∫∫ f du dv into a line integral along the trimming curves of an antiderivative; for a volume bounded by trimmed patches, the divergence theorem first reduces to the patches, then Green's theorem to their trimming curves. (2) "Employ numerical antidifferentiation ... using exponentially-convergent quadrature rules": the antiderivative at each outer quadrature point on a trimming curve is itself computed by a 1-D Gauss rule from a reference line (the "antidifferentiation constants" P_z, P_v, which should be chosen close to the region), so no symbolic antiderivative is needed and arbitrary smooth integrands work. Trimming curves given as polynomial/rational pieces are integrated exactly in parameter; general trimming curves are approximated by cubic pieces and refined. Result (their Table 1): for untrimmed patches or exactly-represented trims, exponential convergence in the number of Gauss points to machine precision; for approximated trims, exponential convergence until the geometric error of the trim approximation dominates, then high-order convergence under geometric refinement. Comparisons against octree-cut-cell Gauss and tetrahedral meshing show far fewer points for the same error; a moment-fitting use is described.
- Limitations: cost ∝ (number of trim pieces) × (outer points) × (inner points); the choice of antidifferentiation constants affects conditioning; rational surfaces are smooth but not polynomial so convergence is exponential rather than exact; no a-priori bound, only the convergence behaviour (use two rule orders to estimate error, as OCCT's adaptive mode does).
- Relevance: `mass` — this is cadrs' current structure (nested Gauss by Green's theorem) with the key change that the *inner* integral is treated as numerical antidifferentiation with its own Gauss order and that the trim pieces are integrated by Gauss in their own parameter (not re-sampled), which is where exponential convergence comes from. The practical recipe to get OCCT-like speed with an error bound: per face, split the uv domain into knot-span cells; cells entirely inside the trimmed region use a degree-matched tensor Gauss rule (exact for polynomial patches, see [M8]); only cells cut by trims use the Green's-theorem path along the trim pieces and the cell edges. For analytic faces (planes, cylinders, cones, spheres, tori) the integrand is trigonometric-polynomial and the inner antiderivative is often closed form.

#### [M8] Gauss rules matched to spline degree: Hughes, Reali & Sangalli 2010; Auricchio et al. 2012
- Citations: Thomas J. R. Hughes, Alessandro Reali, Giancarlo Sangalli. "Efficient quadrature for NURBS-based isogeometric analysis." Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering 199(5-8):301-313, 2010. DOI [10.1016/j.cma.2008.12.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2008.12.004). F. Auricchio, F. Calabrò, T. J. R. Hughes, A. Reali, G. Sangalli. "A simple algorithm for obtaining nearly optimal quadrature rules for NURBS-based isogeometric analysis." CMAME 249-252:15-27, 2012. DOI [10.1016/j.cma.2012.04.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2012.04.014). Both paywalled.
- Content: for integrands that are splines of degree p on a knot vector with C^(p−1) continuity, the optimal ("half-point") rules need about half the degrees of freedom of the spline space in points, independent of p, because the smoothness across knots reduces the dimension; per-span Gauss–Legendre with ⌈(p+1)/2⌉ points is exact but uses roughly twice as many points. Auricchio et al. construct nearly optimal rules by a local nonlinear solve.
- Inference for cadrs (my derivation, not from the papers): for a non-rational B-spline face of bidegree (p,q), the volume integrand x·(S_u × S_v) has bidegree (3p−1, 3q−1) per span and the second-moment integrands x²·n_x have (4p−1, 4q−1); Gauss–Legendre with ⌈3p/2⌉ (resp. 2p) points per span in u and ⌈3q/2⌉ (resp. 2q) in v is therefore *exact* for untrimmed non-rational faces — a fixed rule with a provable zero error, i.e. OCCT's cost model with an error bound. For rational faces the same rule is not exact but the error decays geometrically with the number of points.
- Relevance: `mass` (fixed exact tensor rules per knot span, half-point rules if the span count is large) and `distance`/`silhouette` (any spline-valued objective such as n·w is integrated/sampled exactly with the same logic).

#### [M9] OpenCASCADE GProp / BRepGProp — what it actually does
- Sources: refman https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_g_prop.html (fetched; quotes below), class BRepGProp_Gauss / BRepGProp_VinertGK (https://dev.opencascade.org/doc/occt-7.8.0/refman/html/classBRepGProp__VinertGK.html).
- Documented algorithm: `VolumeProperties(S, Props, Eps, OnlyClosed, SkipShared)`: "Adaptive 2D Gauss integration is used. Parameter Eps sets maximal relative error of computed mass (volume) for each face. Error is calculated as Abs((M(i+1)-M(i))/M(i+1)), M(i+1) and M(i) are values for two successive steps of adaptive integration. Method returns estimation of relative error reached for whole shape." and "WARNING: if Eps > 0.001 algorithm performs non-adaptive integration." The overload without Eps (and `SurfaceProperties`, `LinearProperties`) is the non-adaptive one; it has a `UseTriangulation` flag (integrate over the face triangulation instead of the exact surface). `VolumePropertiesGK(S, Props, Eps=0.001, OnlyClosed, IsUseSpan, CGFlag, IFlag, SkipShared)`: "Adaptive 2D Gauss integration is used ... delivered by the adaptive Gauss-Kronrod method"; "Parameter IsUseSpan says if it is necessary to define spans on a face. This option has an effect only for BSpline faces." `BRepGProp_Gauss` "performs computing of the global inertia properties of geometric object in 3D space by adaptive and non-adaptive 2D Gauss integration algorithms". The README's benchmark corresponds to the non-adaptive overload (11x faster than cadrs, 1e-3 off on blade hubs) and the adaptive overload with Eps = 1e-9 (cadrs is 0.52 of its time).
- From memory of the source (unverified): the non-adaptive path picks a Gauss order from the surface degree (capped by the largest tabulated rule) over the *whole* face domain, and integrates by Green's theorem: outer Gauss points along each edge's pcurve, inner Gauss along the iso-line from the domain's lower bound to that point — i.e. the same Timmer–Stern structure as cadrs, which is why the untrimmed-knot-span rule in cadrs is already more accurate per point.
- Relevance: `mass` — matching OCCT's speed means matching its point counts: a fixed per-span rule of degree-matched order on interior cells ([M8]) and a modest fixed order on the Green's-theorem boundary path, with the adaptive (two-order difference) estimate as the error report.

#### [M10] GPU quadrature for moments
- Citation: Adarsh Krishnamurthy, Sara McMains. "Accurate moment computation using the GPU." Proc. ACM Symposium on Solid and Physical Modeling (SPM 2010), pp. 81-88. DOI [10.1145/1839778.1839790](https://doi.org/10.1145/1839778.1839790) (DOI from memory; not verified). Paywalled; no OA copy found.
- Algorithm (from memory): trimmed NURBS faces are evaluated on the GPU on a fine uv grid with the trim mask; moments are accumulated per grid cell with Gauss points and summed by parallel reduction; cells cut by trims are handled with a boundary correction, and an error bound is derived from the cell size and surface derivative bounds; they report order-of-magnitude speed over CPU with bounded error.
- Relevance: `mass` — only if GPU mass properties are wanted; the error-bound argument (derivative bounds × cell size) applies on the CPU too.

### Inferences
- cadrs' 11x gap to OCCT's fixed rules is a point-count gap, not an algorithmic one; both are Timmer–Stern. The two changes with the best ratio of effort to gain: (1) exact degree-matched tensor rules on knot-span cells wholly inside the trimmed domain (zero error for non-rational faces, [M8]); (2) Gunderman's numerical antidifferentiation along the trim pieces for boundary cells, with the trim pieces integrated in their own parameter rather than re-sampled ([M7]). Report error as the difference between two rule orders (OCCT's definition) so results stay comparable.
- Closed-form exactness ([M5], [M6]) is available for non-rational untrimmed faces and is the right test oracle even if the production path stays quadrature-based.
- Planar faces with straight edges and mesh solids should use the polyhedral closed forms ([M3], [M4]) rather than quadrature.

## Key question 3: Point projection / inversion on NURBS curves and surfaces

### Takeaway
Everything is "prune, then Newton": the differences are the pruning test (control-polygon/convex-hull distance in Ma & Hewitt 2003, end-point half-space and sphere tests in Selimovic 2006, tangent/normal cones in Selimovic 2009 and Johnson & Cohen 2005, circular clipping in Chen et al. 2008, BVH culling in Oh et al. 2012) and the local iteration (first-order Newton in Piegl & Tiller; second-order curvature-based steps in Hu & Wallner 2005, proven cubic for inversion). For a kernel with exact analytic surfaces, the pruning step should be the per-span normal-cone test and the iteration Hu–Wallner's.

### Cited Findings

#### [P1] Piegl & Tiller 1997 — the textbook Newton projection
- Citation: Les Piegl, Wayne Tiller. *The NURBS Book*, 2nd ed., Springer 1997, §6.1 "Point inversion and projection for curves and surfaces" (pp. 229-234). DOI [10.1007/978-3-642-59223-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-59223-2). Book; no OA.
- Algorithm: sample the curve (or surface) coarsely (per span, n₀ points), take the closest sample as the start, then Newton on f(u) = C'(u)·(C(u) − P) = 0, u_{i+1} = u_i − f/f' with f' = C''·(C−P) + |C'|²; for surfaces a 2×2 Newton on (S_u·(S−P), S_v·(S−P)) with the Jacobian from second derivatives; stop on point coincidence ε₁, zero cosine ε₂ (angle between C−P and the tangent), or no parameter change; clamp to the domain (wrap for closed curves).
- Limitations: converges to the nearest *stationary* point of the distance from the start sample, so the sampling density decides correctness; no guarantee of the global minimum; needs second derivatives.
- Relevance: `nurbs`/`distance` — cadrs' damped Newton with second derivatives is this; the improvements below are in the start selection and the step.

#### [P2] Ma & Hewitt 2003 — control polygon approach
- Citation: Ying Liang Ma, W. T. Hewitt. "Point inversion and projection for NURBS curve and surface: Control polygon approach." Computer Aided Geometric Design 20(2):79-99, 2003. DOI [10.1016/S0167-8396(03)00021-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-8396(03)00021-9). Conference version: TPCG 2003, IEEE, https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1206938. Paywalled; UEA/Coventry repository records exist but no OA file was found.
- Algorithm (abstract + secondary): decompose the NURBS into Bézier pieces by knot insertion; for each piece test the point against the control polygon/net using the strong convex hull property (distance from P to the control polygon bounds the distance to the piece; pieces whose lower bound exceeds the current best upper bound are discarded); for inversion, the candidate pieces are those whose convex hull contains P; the surviving pieces are refined by Newton–Raphson from the closest control-polygon point.
- Limitations: Chen, Su, Yong, Paul & Sun, "A counterexample on point inversion and projection for NURBS curve", CAGD 24(5):302, 2007 shows a case where the candidate test misses the true piece (the point lies closer to a piece whose control polygon is farther).
- Relevance: `nurbs` — per-span convex-hull pruning before Newton; cheap because cadrs' B-splines are already stored per span.

#### [P3] Selimovic 2006 / 2009 — improved subdivision criteria; tangent cones
- Citations: Ilijas Selimovic. "Improved algorithms for the projection of points on NURBS curves and surfaces." CAGD 23(5):439-445, 2006. DOI [10.1016/j.cagd.2006.01.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cagd.2006.01.007). Selimovic. "On NURBS algorithms using tangent cones." CAGD 26(7):772-778, 2009. DOI [10.1016/j.cagd.2009.03.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cagd.2009.03.004). Both paywalled.
- Algorithm (abstract, secondary sources, memory): 2006 adds two exclusion tests before subdividing a Bézier piece: (i) if all control points lie on the far side of the plane through an end point perpendicular to (P − end point), the closest point of the piece is that end point (so the piece need not be subdivided, "selective subdivision based on the distance to the end/corner point"); (ii) a piece can be discarded if its control polygon is entirely outside the sphere of the current best distance. 2009 uses the tangent cone of a piece (the cone spanned by the control-polygon direction vectors): if the cone of directions from P to the control points and the tangent cone are such that the projection equation (C − P)·C' = 0 cannot change sign, the piece has at most one stationary point and Newton can be run safely; this gives a termination guarantee for subdivision. Chen et al. later observed that Selimovic's test needs all control points on one side of the hyperplane, which motivated circular clipping [P5].
- Relevance: `nurbs` — the end-point half-space test and the tangent-cone uniqueness test are the two cheapest robust pre-tests for cadrs' Newton.

#### [P4] Hu & Wallner 2005 — second-order projection
- Citation: Shi-Min Hu, Johannes Wallner. "A second order algorithm for orthogonal projection onto curves and surfaces." CAGD 22(3):251-260, 2005. DOI [10.1016/j.cagd.2004.12.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cagd.2004.12.001). OA copy listed by Semantic Scholar on Wallner's site: http://www.geometrie.tugraz.at/wallner/sproj.pdf (the TU Graz server refused connections during this session; retry later). Convergence analysis: Xiaowu Li et al., "Convergence analysis on a second order algorithm for orthogonal projection onto curves", Symmetry 9(10):210, 2017, OA https://www.mdpi.com/2073-8994/9/10/210 (MDPI blocked the PDF download) — they prove second-order convergence of the projection step for planar curves independent of the start value and third-order for the inversion step.
- Algorithm (from memory): instead of Newton on the stationarity equation, at the current foot point q = C(t) build the local second-order model: the osculating circle (curve) or the normal-section circle in the step direction (surface); project P onto the circle, map the arc length back to a parameter increment Δt = s/|C'| (corrected by the curvature), and iterate. For surfaces the step direction is the projection of P − q onto the tangent plane, the normal curvature κ_n in that direction gives the circle. Each step uses first and second derivatives like Newton but is geometrically motivated and converges from farther away; it is the "geometric iteration" that later papers (Li et al. 2017, "hybrid second-order" variants) build on.
- Limitations: needs curvature; degenerate where κ = 0 (falls back to first order); same local-minimum caveat as Newton.
- Relevance: `nurbs`/`distance` — a drop-in replacement for the damped Newton iteration with better basin behaviour; cadrs already evaluates all second derivatives, so the cost is the same.

#### [P5] Chen et al. 2008 — circular clipping (point–curve); Chen et al. 2009 algebraic
- Citations: Xiao-Diao Chen, Jun-Hai Yong, Guozhao Wang, Jean-Claude Paul, Gang Xu. "Computing the minimum distance between a point and a NURBS curve." - Algorithm (abstract): subdivide the curve and discard the parts outside the circle centred at P whose radius is the current best distance (test by convex hull of the piece against the circle); the radius shrinks as closer points are found; "a simple termination condition keeps the number of subdivision steps very small"; final Newton. The algebraic variant forms the polynomial (C − P)·C' of degree 2n − 1 in Bernstein form and isolates its roots (all stationary points) rather than iterating from guesses.
- Relevance: `nurbs` — circular/spherical clipping is the natural pruning for cadrs' branch and bound (upper bound = best so far; lower bound = distance to the piece's hull), and the algebraic route gives *all* stationary points for low degree (useful for maximum distance too).

#### [P6] Oh, Kim, Lee, Kim & Elber 2012 — BVH culling; Coons BVH
- Citations: Young-Taek Oh, Yong-Joon Kim, Jieun Lee, Myung-Soo Kim, Gershon Elber. "Efficient point-projection to freeform curves and surfaces." - Algorithm (abstract + memory): a hierarchy of tight bounding volumes over the curve/surface (for surfaces, a Coons patch approximation per node with a bound on its distance to the surface, so the BVH is compact and the bounds tight); culling eliminates nodes whose lower bound exceeds the best upper bound and nodes whose normal cone shows no projection can occur; the remaining subproblems are solved numerically; for planar "spiral" curves (monotone curvature, no inflection) a few geometric tests guarantee convergence of the numerical method to the closest point. The BVH is also used for continuous (moving-point) projection and Hausdorff distance (Kim et al., CAD 2013).
- Relevance: `distance`/`nurbs` — the modern form of cadrs' cell boxes: per-span boxes with a normal cone give both the lower bound and the "no stationary point here" prune.

#### [P7] Algebraic point projection for low-degree NURBS (2020)
- Citation: Huanyu Liao, Pavan Kumar Vaitheeswaran, Tao Song, Ganesh Subbarayan. "Algebraic point projection for immersed boundary analysis on low degree NURBS curves and surfaces." - Algorithm (abstract): for quadratic/cubic pieces the projection condition is a low-degree polynomial whose roots are found algebraically (closed form / companion matrix), giving all candidate foot points without iteration; used for immersed-boundary analysis where many projections per element are needed.
- Relevance: `nurbs` — for degree ≤ 3 spans an all-roots solve is competitive with iteration and is exhaustive.

#### [P8] GPU projection: Krishnamurthy et al. 2009
- Citation: Adarsh Krishnamurthy, Rahul Khardekar, Sara McMains, Kirk Haller, Gershon Elber. "Performing efficient NURBS modeling operations on the GPU." IEEE TVCG 15(4):530-543, 2009. DOI [10.1109/TVCG.2009.29](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2009.29) (Crossref confirms). - cadrs' Newton with all second derivatives is the standard local step; replacing it with Hu–Wallner's curvature step [P4] is cheap and better conditioned, and the per-span pre-tests of [P3]/[P5] remove most false starts. The counterexample of Chen et al. 2007 shows convex-hull-only pruning is not sufficient; keep a global bound (sphere of the best distance) as the prune.

## Key question 4: Minimum distance between surfaces/bodies

### Takeaway
Two families: geometric branch-and-bound on hierarchies of bounds (Johnson & Cohen 1998 LUB-tree with convex hulls of B-spline pieces and refinement; OBB/sphere trees for polygons; Coons BVH), and algebraic event enumeration (Elber & Grandine 2008: solve the antipodal/normal-alignment constraints by a multivariate subdivision solver; Chen et al. 2006 for implicit surfaces). cadrs' branch-and-bound on cell boxes then Newton is in the first family; the published refinements are breadth-first search with early upper bounds, tangent-cone pruning of cells that cannot contain an extremum, time coherence of bounds, and enumeration of boundary events (edges, vertices) separately from interior antipodal points.

### Cited Findings

#### [D1] Johnson & Cohen 1998 — LUB-tree framework (MUST READ); 1999 bound coherence; 2005 tangent cones
- Citations: David E. Johnson, Elaine Cohen. "A framework for efficient minimum distance computations." Proc. IEEE ICRA 1998, vol. 4, pp. 3678-3684. DOI [10.1109/ROBOT.1998.681403](https://doi.org/10.1109/ROBOT.1998.681403). OA (Utah): https://www2.cs.utah.edu/gdc/publications/papers/johnson98a.pdf — local `Johnson-Cohen-1998-framework-efficient-minimum-distance.pdf` (verified). Johnson & Cohen. "Bound coherence for minimum distance computations." Proc. IEEE ICRA 1999. OA: https://www-old.cs.utah.edu/~dejohnso/publications/icra99.pdf — local `Johnson-Cohen-1999-bound-coherence-minimum-distance.pdf`. Tech report UUCS-97-003 "Minimum distance queries for polygonal and parametric models" (abstract only at Utah; DTIC copy ADA440300 download failed). Johnson & Cohen, "Distance extrema for spline models using tangent cones", Graphics Interface 2005 (no OA copy found; Utah project page https://www-old.cs.utah.edu/gdc/projects/minimum_distance/).
- Algorithm (from the 1998 PDF, section 3): every surface representation provides five operations: bounding-volume generation, a lower bound on distance between two nodes, an upper bound on the model minimum distance, bounding-volume refinement, and a termination test. Pruning loop: (1) for each *active pair* of nodes compute the lower bound from the bounding volumes; (2) establish an upper bound (distance between cached model points: vertices for polygons; for B-splines, the closest points of the two convex hulls of the saved best pair mapped onto the surfaces by bilinear interpolation between the parametric nodes of the control mesh); (3) prune every active pair whose lower bound exceeds the upper bound; (4) split survivors by refinement; (5) repeat until termination. For B-spline surfaces the initial nodes are the polynomial pieces with their local convex hulls (Gilbert's algorithm gives the hull–hull distance, after a quick AABB test); refinement groups surviving pieces into contiguous "active regions", extracts each region as its own surface by knot insertion at the region boundary and refines it (so already-pruned regions are not refined), with a granularity limit on region size to avoid diagonal-strip pathologies; the control mesh converges quadratically to the surface, so the upper bound converges in a few iterations. Breadth-first search is shown to beat depth-first and gives time-critical behaviour (bounds early). Reported: 10⁻⁶ parametric precision at 10-50 Hz for point–surface and 2-20 Hz surface–surface on an SGI Indigo2, versus ~1 s for the numerical (root-finding) formulation; stable up to degree 7. Complexity: best case O(log N) bound evaluations per model, typical bad cases (parallel flats, concentric spheres) ~2N lower-bound and log N upper-bound evaluations. 1999: cache the previous frame's bounds and reuse them as the initial upper bound and pruning threshold ("more than double the speed"). 2005 (from memory): a node whose tangent cone and the cone of directions to the other node's bound do not admit a perpendicular configuration cannot contain a distance extremum, so it is pruned even if its distance bound is small; this also enumerates *all* local extrema (needed for maximum distance).
- Limitations: convex hulls of control points are loose for high degree/few spans; refinement by knot insertion allocates; breadth-first needs memory for the active-pair list; the method finds the global minimum but with no closed-form error, it stops at a tolerance on (upper − lower).
- Relevance: `distance` — the direct improvement of cadrs' "branch and bound on cell boxes": (a) upper bounds from surface points early (breadth-first), (b) convex hulls (or OBBs of control nets) instead of AABB cell boxes for the lower bound, (c) tangent-cone pruning so Newton is only launched where an extremum can exist, (d) reuse of bounds between queries on the same bodies.

#### [D2] Elber & Grandine 2008 — Hausdorff and minimal distances by algebraic events
- Citation: Gershon Elber, Tom Grandine. "Hausdorff and minimal distances between parametric freeforms in R² and R³." Geometric Modeling and Processing 2008, LNCS 4975, pp. 191-204. DOI [10.1007/978-3-540-79246-8_15](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-79246-8_15) (Semantic Scholar confirms). OA (author's site): https://gershon.cs.technion.ac.il/papers/HausdorffDistance.pdf — local `Elber-Grandine-2008-Hausdorff-minimal-distances-freeforms.pdf` (verified). Follow-ups: Son, Kim & Elber, "Precise Hausdorff distance computation for freeform surfaces based on computations with osculating toroidal patches", CAGD 2021; Kim, Oh, Yoon, Kim, Elber, "Efficient Hausdorff distance computation for freeform geometric models in close proximity", CAD 45(2), 2013.
- Algorithm (from the PDF, sections 2-4): enumerate every *event* at which an extreme distance can occur and express each as a square system of algebraic (piecewise polynomial/rational) constraints in the curves'/surfaces' parameters: end-point vs end-point; a normal line of one curve through an end point of the other, ⟨C₁(r₀) − C₂(t), C₂'(t)⟩ = 0 (one equation, one unknown); interior antipodal points, ⟨C₁ − C₂, C₁'⟩ = 0 and ⟨C₁ − C₂, C₂'⟩ = 0 (two equations, two unknowns); for surfaces in R³, ⟨S₁ − S₂, ∂S₁/∂u⟩ = ⟨S₁ − S₂, ∂S₁/∂v⟩ = ⟨S₁ − S₂, ∂S₂/∂s⟩ = ⟨S₁ − S₂, ∂S₂/∂t⟩ = 0 (four in four) plus boundary-curve-vs-surface and corner events; for the Hausdorff distance additional bisector-type events (a point equidistant from two locations of the other shape). All systems are solved by Elber's multivariate subdivision solver (convex-hull exclusion on the B-spline representation of each constraint, subdivision, Newton termination), which returns all solutions; the minimal/Hausdorff distance is the min/max over the finite event set. Exact (to tolerance), representation-aware (the constraints are B-splines, products formed symbolically).
- Limitations: the solver's cost is exponential in the number of variables (4 for surface–surface is fine; 6+ for moving objects is slow); symbolic products raise degrees (products of degree-3 splines give degree-6 constraint fields); needs the whole surface, not a trimmed domain (trims are handled by post-filtering solutions in uv).
- Relevance: `distance` (max distance / Hausdorff: cadrs' `max_distance` needs the event enumeration, since the maximum is attained at antipodal pairs or on boundaries, which branch and bound alone finds slowly), `silhouette` (the same solver attitude: n·w = 0 as a B-spline function), `nurbs` (symbolic products, cf. [M6]).

#### [D3] Chen, Yong, Zheng, Paul & Sun 2006 — minimum distance between two implicit algebraic surfaces
- Citation: Xiao-Diao Chen, Jun-Hai Yong, Guo-Qin Zheng, Jean-Claude Paul, Jia-Guang Sun. "Computing minimum distance between two implicit algebraic surfaces." Computer-Aided Design 38(10):1053-1061, 2006. DOI [10.1016/j.cad.2006.04.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2006.04.012) (Crossref confirms authors). OA listed by Semantic Scholar: https://inria.hal.science/inria-00518397v1/file/Xiao-DiaoChen2006a.pdf (HAL's bot check blocked the download; retry in a browser).
- Algorithm (from memory): the minimum distance between two implicit surfaces f = 0 and g = 0 occurs where the connecting line is normal to both: ∇f(p) ∥ ∇g(q) ∥ (p − q); the paper turns this into a system in the two points' coordinates and solves it by a combination of an offset/"line-geometry" formulation and a subdivision-plus-Newton scheme, with a proof that each local minimum is found and a global bound by an interval-style test; it also treats the parametric–implicit case by substitution.
- Limitations: implicit algebraic surfaces of low degree (quadrics, tori are the practical cases); for two parametric surfaces the system is 4-D as in [D2].
- Relevance: `distance` — for cadrs' analytic surfaces (planes, quadrics, tori all have cheap implicit forms) the normal-alignment system is small and can be solved directly instead of by cell boxes: e.g. cylinder–cylinder or torus–plane distance has closed-form or 1-D event sets.

## Key question 5: Hidden-line removal and silhouettes for engineering drawings

### Takeaway
Exact HLR on free-form B-reps follows one recipe since Appel 1967 / Hornung 1985 / Elber & Cohen 1990: collect the "curves of interest" (sharp edges, boundary/trim edges, silhouettes = zero set of n·w, optionally iso-lines), split them at their mutual crossings in the projection (and at cusps), decide visibility of each piece with one ray/point test against the faces, then propagate Appel's quantitative invisibility along the curve, changing it only at crossings with silhouette/boundary curves. OCCT's HLRBRep_Algo is this recipe implemented with edge-vs-face 2D intersections for every pair ("comparing each edge of the shape to be visualized with each of its faces"), which is exact but O(E·F). Hertzmann & Zorin 2000 and Bénard/Hertzmann/Kass 2014 supply the robust silhouette topology (dual-surface search, cusp detection, consistent orientation); Seong/Elber/Cohen 2006 give the all-view-directions algebraic formulation.

### Cited Findings

#### [H1] Appel 1967 — quantitative invisibility
- Citation: Arthur Appel. "The notion of quantitative invisibility and the machine rendering of solids." Proc. 22nd ACM National Conference, pp. 387-393, 1967. DOI [10.1145/800196.806007](https://doi.org/10.1145/800196.806007) (ACM lists the PDF as free-to-read "bronze"; the download was blocked by ACM's bot check).
- Algorithm: for each line (edge) of a polyhedral scene, its quantitative invisibility QI(s) = number of front-facing faces between the eye and the point at arc length s; QI changes only where the projected line crosses the projection of a *contour* edge (an edge between a front and a back face, i.e. a silhouette edge), by ±1 depending on which side of the contour the line passes; so compute QI at one point by a ray test, walk the line through its crossings with contour edges updating QI, and draw the pieces with QI = 0. Cost: crossings of E lines with C contour edges (C ≪ E for smooth bodies) plus one ray test per line (or per connected component, since QI propagates through vertices with a correction for the faces incident to the vertex).
- Limitations: polyhedra; needs robust crossing detection and vertex rules; degenerate (coincident, tangential) crossings need care; "superimposed lines" are not removed.
- Relevance: `hlr` — the propagation rule replaces one ray test per piece by one per connected chain; cadrs' "visibility by rays against the faces" per piece is the slow but robust variant. The Bénard & Hertzmann tutorial §4.7 restates the rule with the modern singular-point list.

#### [H2] Weiler & Atherton 1977 — polygon-area sorting
- Citation: Kevin Weiler, Peter Atherton. "Hidden surface removal using polygon area sorting." Proc. SIGGRAPH 1977 (Computer Graphics 11(2):214-222). DOI [10.1145/563858.563896](https://doi.org/10.1145/563858.563896). Paywalled.
- Algorithm: image-space-exact *hidden surface* removal by clipping polygons against each other in the projection plane (the Weiler–Atherton polygon clipper, recursive on the "inside" pieces), sorted by depth; produces visible polygon fragments, from which visible edges follow.
- Limitations: polygons only; the clipping is quadratic and numerically delicate; not a line algorithm.
- Relevance: `hlr` — background only; cadrs' PolyAlgo-style path (if any) would use a z-buffer or BVH ray tests instead.

#### [H3] Elber & Cohen 1990 — hidden curve removal for free-form surfaces (MUST READ with [H6])
- Citation: Gershon Elber, Elaine Cohen. "Hidden curve removal for free form surfaces." Proc. SIGGRAPH 1990, Computer Graphics 24(4):95-104. DOI [10.1145/97879.97890](https://doi.org/10.1145/97879.97890) (ACM "bronze" free-to-read page https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/97879.97890; download blocked by ACM's bot check; Technion/Utah OA paths tried returned 404/403). SIGGRAPH history record https://history.siggraph.org/?p=122052.
- Algorithm (abstract + the account in Seong et al. 2006 + memory): the "curves of interest" on NURBS (possibly trimmed) surfaces are boundary curves, silhouette curves (the zero set of the scalar B-spline function ⟨n(u,v), V⟩, formed symbolically as a spline by products of the surface's partial derivatives and contoured by subdivision/marching), iso-parametric curves and curves along C¹ discontinuities; all curves are projected to the view plane and intersected pairwise in 2D (curve–curve intersections, with surface coherence to limit the pairs tested); each resulting piece is tested for visibility once by a point test: the test point's (x,y) is inverted on each candidate surface (solving two equations in u,v) to compare depths, so a piece is hidden if any surface lies in front of it; this is Hornung et al.'s 1985 extension of Appel's QI from polyhedra to parametric patches, applied to NURBS and extended to trimmed surfaces. Output is exact curves ("most of the output set has an exact representation"), unlike polygon-based HLR whose silhouettes are polylines.
- Limitations: pairwise 2D intersections of all curves is O(n²) without a spatial index; the per-piece point test needs reliable surface inversion; QI propagation is not used (one test per piece); tangential/overlapping curves are special cases.
- Relevance: `hlr` and `silhouette` — this is cadrs' current design in its original form; the gain is in the silhouette extraction as a spline zero set (exact topology, not marching squares in uv) and in using surface coherence (which curves can hide which) to limit ray tests.

#### [H4] Hertzmann & Zorin 2000 — dual-surface silhouettes, cusps, segmentation (MUST READ)
- Citation: Aaron Hertzmann, Denis Zorin. "Illustrating smooth surfaces." Proc. SIGGRAPH 2000, pp. 517-526. DOI [10.1145/344779.345074](https://doi.org/10.1145/344779.345074). OA (author's page): https://cs.nyu.edu/~dzorin/papers/hertzmann2000iss.pdf — local `Hertzmann-Zorin-2000-illustrating-smooth-surfaces.pdf` (verified); project page https://mrl.cs.nyu.edu/publications/illustrating-smooth/.
- Algorithm (from the PDF, §4): the silhouette set is the zero set of g(p) = n(p)·(p − c) on the surface; on a mesh approximating a smooth surface they compute true normals at vertices, interpolate g linearly over triangles and take its zero set (segments through edge interiors), which gives silhouette chains with the *topology of a smooth surface* (closed loops or chains ending on feature lines; the sign is perturbed at exact zeros for general position) rather than the complex cusps of polygonal silhouettes. Fast detection (§4.3): the dual surface M' (images of tangent planes under the duality map, points on the unit sphere / in homogeneous coordinates) is built once; for a viewpoint C the silhouette is the intersection of the plane C·x = 0 with M'; the dual-surface triangles are stored in an octree (handling points at infinity by using the sphere), so each view costs a plane-vs-octree traversal, output-sensitive, for both orthographic and perspective views. Cusps (§4.2): the projected silhouette's only stable singularity is the simple cusp, found as the intersection of the zero sets of g and of a "cusp function" built from the principal curvatures and the view direction (c₁ = v·w₁, c₂ = v·w₂, gradient ∇g = [−2κ₁c₁, −2κ₂c₂]); robust against spurious cusps. Visibility (§4.4): silhouette curves are split at cusps, silhouette–feature crossings and silhouette–silhouette image crossings; one ray test per segment, then propagation.
- Limitations: a mesh with true normals/curvatures is the input (the smooth surface is inferred), so for exact B-rep the zero-set extraction should be done on the surface's own n·w; cusp detection needs curvatures; the dual-space octree is a per-model precomputation.
- Relevance: `silhouette` (exact cusp detection on parametric faces, which decides where visibility can change, and the dual-space idea for many views of one model, e.g. all drawing views at once) and `hlr` (the segmentation list before ray tests).

#### [H5] Bénard, Hertzmann & Kass 2014 — contours with accurate topology
- Citation: Pierre Bénard, Aaron Hertzmann, Michael Kass. "Computing smooth surface contours with accurate topology." ACM TOG 33(2), article 19, 2014. DOI [10.1145/2558307](https://doi.org/10.1145/2558307) (Crossref confirms). No OA copy found (author-page guesses 404).
- Algorithm (from memory): for subdivision/smooth surfaces, extract interpolated contours as in [H4], then detect and repair topological inconsistencies between the contour's image-space orientation and the surface's front/back classification by local refinement of the mesh near the contour (so that every contour segment is consistently oriented and cusps are exactly where the contour's image tangent is parallel to the view ray); then QI propagation with the singular points (cusps, bitangent/image crossings, contour–boundary crossings) enumerated; produces contours whose visibility is correct for stylised line rendering of smooth surfaces.
- Relevance: `silhouette`/`hlr` — the checklist of singular points at which QI changes, and the consistency condition (contour orientation vs front/back) that can be used as a validation of cadrs' marching-squares silhouettes.

#### [H6] Bénard & Hertzmann 2019 — Line drawings from 3D models: a tutorial (MUST READ)
- Citation: Pierre Bénard, Aaron Hertzmann. "Line Drawings from 3D Models: A Tutorial." Foundations and Trends in Computer Graphics and Vision 11(1-2):1-159, 2019. DOI [10.1561/0600000075](https://doi.org/10.1561/0600000075). OA: arXiv:1810.01175, https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.01175 — local `Benard-Hertzmann-2019-line-drawings-from-3D-models-tutorial.pdf` (verified; 16.9 MB).
- Content (from the PDF's table of contents): ch. 3 mesh contours (definition, generic position, sparsity, extraction algorithms); ch. 4 mesh curve visibility: ray tests (§4.1), concave/convex edges, singular points (§4.3: cusps, curtain folds, bitangent/image-space intersections, boundary–contour crossings), view graph data structures, curve-based visibility algorithms, quantitative invisibility (§4.7, Appel), planar maps (§4.8); ch. 5 hardware-based extraction and visibility (two-pass rendering, item buffer, segment atlas); ch. 6 smooth surfaces as meshes (interpolated contours, planar maps); ch. 7 parametric surfaces: contour definition as n·v = 0 in uv, extraction (marching in the parameter domain, as cadrs does), contour curvature, singular points, visibility computation; ch. 8 implicit surfaces; ch. 9 stylisation. It is the only source that lays out, side by side, the exact (curve-based, QI) and the image-space (item buffer) approaches and their failure modes.
- Relevance: `hlr`/`silhouette` — use ch. 4 and 7 as the specification for which points a silhouette must be split at before visibility is decided, and for the ray-test-plus-propagation design.

#### [H7] Seong, Elber & Cohen 2006 — simultaneous precise visibility of sculptured models
- Citation: Joon-Kyung Seong, Gershon Elber, Elaine Cohen. "Simultaneous precise solutions to the visibility problem of sculptured models." Geometric Modeling and Processing 2006, LNCS 4077, pp. 451-464. DOI [10.1007/11802914_32](https://doi.org/10.1007/11802914_32) (Crossref confirms). OA (Utah): https://www2.cs.utah.edu/gdc/publications/papers/seong-visibility.pdf — local `Seong-Elber-Cohen-2006-visibility-sculptured-surfaces.pdf` (verified).
- Algorithm (from the PDF): visibility is lifted to a higher-dimensional parameter space so that all view directions are solved at once: for planar curves, a point C(t) is hidden from direction θ if some C(r) lies on the ray — two polynomial equations (one an inequality) in (t, r, θ) whose zero set is a 2-manifold in 3-D parameter space; the hidden set is its projection onto the tθ-plane, whose boundary is found by solving three polynomial constraints in three variables; the result is a *visibility chart* (visible parts of the boundary for every direction). For surfaces, 3 equations in 6 variables (two surface parameters each, two view angles) whose zero set is a 4-manifold, with the same projection argument. All solved with the NURBS convex-hull/subdivision multivariate solver; no piecewise-linear approximation. Related work discussion traces the exact-curve lineage: Appel → Hornung et al. 1985 (bi-quadratic patches) → Elber & Cohen 1990 (NURBS, trimmed) → Nishita's Bézier clipping → Krishnan & Manocha.
- Limitations: high-dimensional solver cost (6 variables for surfaces); for one fixed view a direct 2-D/4-D formulation is cheaper; output is in parameter space (needs mapping to drawing curves).
- Relevance: `hlr` — the exact formulation to validate against, and the right tool if cadrs ever computes visibility charts (e.g. all standard drawing views, or mould-draw/accessibility analysis) rather than one view at a time.

#### [H8] OpenCASCADE HLRBRep_Algo / HLRBRep_PolyAlgo
- Sources: refman https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_h_l_r_b_rep___algo.html (fetched); modeling-algorithms user guide https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__modeling_algos.html (HLR section fetched); bug 0023836 (missing contour lines in 6.5.3) https://tracker.dev.opencascade.org/view.php?id=23836.
- Documented algorithm: "based on the principle of comparing each edge of the shape to be visualized with each of its faces"; workflow Projector → Add(shape) → Update ("compute the outlines of the shape", via HLRTopoBRep_OutLiner, which inserts the apparent contours (from memory: computed by the Contap package, analytic for quadrics, numeric marching otherwise) as new edges into the face topology) → Hide (visibility of every edge against every face: 2D intersections of the projected edge with the projected face boundary, depth comparison at each interference, then intervals of visible/hidden along the edge, with the initial status "depending of the back faces of a closed shell"); results extracted by HLRBRep_HLRToShape as visible/hidden sharp, smooth, sewn, outline (silhouette) and iso-parametric edges; "When you use HLRBRep_Algo, you obtain an exact result", whereas HLRBRep_PolyAlgo "works on a polyhedral simplification ... reduce the computation time, but obtain polygonal segments"; "No smoothing algorithm is provided." Documented limitations: "Superimposed lines are not eliminated by this algorithm", "There must be no unfinished objects inside the shape", points and infinite faces are not processed, complex shapes may be slow (the bug tracker has open reports of missing contour lines).
- Relevance: `hlr` — cadrs' design is equivalent; OCCT's weaknesses (all-pairs edge/face interference, no superposition removal) are the places where a spatial index over projected curves and a QI walk would make cadrs faster than OCCT rather than merely equal.

### Inferences
- cadrs' `hlr` already has the exact-curve ingredients; the published improvements are: (1) split silhouettes at cusps (computed from curvature, [H4]) and at image-space crossings *before* visibility; (2) one ray test per chain plus Appel's QI propagation instead of per-piece ray tests; (3) a 2D spatial index (or sweep) over projected curves so crossing detection is not all-pairs; (4) for B-spline faces, extract n·w = 0 as a spline zero set ([H3], [D2]) instead of marching squares when exact topology matters.
